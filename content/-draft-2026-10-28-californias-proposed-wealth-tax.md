So I won't be publishing this. It was more of a thought exercise for myself. Turns out I'm not really well versed in economics, and I made some significant errors in the presentation of facts in this piece. ## Preface

California is voting on [Proposition 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_California_Proposition_40), a "one-time 5% tax on the net worth of the California residents worth over $1 billion."

I think there is probably some legitimate nuance to this bill, and I'm not knowledgeable enough in policy to make a determination about whether or not it is "good" or "right". For example, I could be sympathetic to the argument that we need a more sustainable and long-term solution to combat wealth inequality rather than a one-time tax with some legal ambiguities.

But when I found myself thinking about possible reasons that billionaires might oppose this bill, the two main reasons I could conjure are:

1. Billionaires just don't want to pay it
2. Billionaires believe their wealth is legitimate, and any attempt to take back part of that wealth is illegitimate

I'm curious about the second point. I imagine that many billionaires percieve themselves as "job creators" who are doing a net good for society. They might believe that billions of dollars is the correct amount of compensation for creating businesses that employ so many people and create value in the economy. Creating jobs is great, but I am suspicious of the claim that one should earn a billion dollars by employing people.

How much money does a founder have to make per employee in order to become a billionaire?

## Back-of-the-envelope math

Let's take the example of Sergey Brin, co-founder of Google, who currently has a net worth of about US$250 billion. Why Sergey? A) He has contributed over $100 million to defeating Proposition 40, and B) he co-founded a large public company that we can use to estimate the wealth he has extracted from his employees.

[Current](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/googl/employees/) [estimates](https://www.reveliolabs.com/companies/google/employees) suggest that Google has around 195,000 employees, but that number has grown considerably over time. See [Footnotes](#footnotes) for year-by-year calculation. This gives us a total of 1,961,026 "employee years" that Google has employed in the history of the company.

To accumulate $250 billion in wealth, that means that Sergey Brin has earned $127,484.28 per employee per year of Google's operations. And consider: Sergey Brin is just one of two co-founders! Using this framework, we can say that nearly $250,000 of wealth was extracted from every employee every year over the history of the company, and that wealth now rests in the hands of two people. Is $127,000/year a fair price to pay to Sergey Brin for creating jobs for the rest of us? I'll leave this as an exercise for the reader.

## My uneducated economic theory

I'm basing this math on the idea that Sergey Brin's net worth is the result of extracting wealth from his employees. This is not a universal truth; rather, it is one perspective through which we can view economic history, based on my (probably poor) understanding of Marxism.

One way to think about it is that businesses make money by producing products or services which are sold for profit. The business distributes through profits to its employees through salaries. We could imagine that every employee earns a salary which is proportional to the amount of profit they generate for the business, based on the labor they provide to produce the products the company sells. (This is rarely literally true, but we can use it to frame our thinking.) Business owners do not produce products directly - they are involved in business operations. Therefore, the business owner's salary is not proportional to the labor they are providing, but rather proportional to the labor that their employees are providing. The business owner extracts some portion of pay from each profit-producing employee in order to sustain the business operations.

My thinking is this: Google has created value for the world through its products. Google charges money for its products, which result in profit. That profit is distributed to employees through pay for the labor they provide while producing the products. If all the profits of Google are created from its products, then each employee earns a portion of those profits based on the value they provide towards producing the products. Sergey Brin's value does not come from directly producing the products; rather, he runs the business operations, which indirectly produce the products which produce profit. Since Sergey's labor does not go directly to producing products, his pay has to come from profits that would otherwise go to people producing the products. He is extracting his wealth from the work of his employees. That isn't to say this type of wealth extraction is bad or wrong, its simply one way of viewing the flow of capital through this business.

There are also arguments to be made about the importance of inventing the technologies that made Google profitable and powerful, and the value of running a business in a way that leads to the ability to create 1,961,026 "employee-years" of labor potential. That is hard so I'm not going to do it. There's a reason the section above is called "back-of-the-envelope math", it isn't meant to be highly precise. My goal here is to take one simplistic perspective and extrapolate it to its logical conclusion of how someone could become a multi-billionaire.
